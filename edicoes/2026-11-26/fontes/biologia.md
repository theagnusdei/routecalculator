Evolution's balancing act—keeping genes in sync after genome duplication
Getting the right balance is important in all walks of life, whether it's a healthy combination of food groups in a balanced diet or a balanced mix of skills that allows a work or sports team to succeed.
Cells face a similar challenge—thousands of genes work together to control cell behavior, and the proteins they produce often need to be present in the correct proportions.
But what happens when the entire genetic code is suddenly doubled, and each copy has the opportunity to evolve independently? How does evolution maintain correct proportions of gene products as individual gene copies evolve and diverge?
A new article by researchers at the Norwegian University of Life Sciences has shed important light on this exact question, indicating that many duplicated genes maintain a connection in how they are regulated, helping to preserve the balance of gene activity across cells. The paper is published in the journal Science Advances.
The dosage-balance challenge after whole-genome duplication
Whole-genome duplication (WGD) is an evolutionary event in which an organism acquires a second copy of its entire genome.
"Ancient WGDs have contributed to the evolution of most eukaryotic life, including humans; we are descendants of two WGDs early in vertebrate evolution," explains the leader of the study, Marie Saitou.
After WGD, all genes have an identical partner, each surrounded by identical DNA sequences that control when, and to what extent, the genes are active. The two gene copies begin with matching sequences and regulatory surroundings, and so the relative proportions of all the proteins encoded by the genes are initially maintained.
However, across millions of years, mutations gradually change the protein-coding potential of genes and their regulation, and some genes even take on new roles or disappear entirely from the genome. This can dramatically impact the dosage balance of protein levels across the cell.
Evolutionary biologists have long been interested in dosage balance theory. Since many proteins do not act alone, but instead as part of large molecular 'teams' in signaling pathways, protein complexes and gene-regulatory networks, evolution may favor mechanisms that help preserve balanced proportions of these proteins.
One way evolution could help maintain such balance after WGD is through dosage compensation.
"Here, a change in activity of one gene copy is offset by an opposing change in the other copy. If one duplicate increases activity, the other becomes less active, helping to stabilize their combined output," says Saitou.
However, the underlying regulatory mechanisms that maintain dosage balance over millions of years following WGD remain poorly understood.
Atlantic salmon as a valuable natural tool to study dosage compensation
Atlantic salmon provide a unique opportunity for studying WGD and dosage compensation, because their lineage experienced WGD relatively recently (80 to 100 million years ago), and many of the duplicated genes are still present today.
In the new study, NMBU researchers used large-scale genomic data from Atlantic salmon to compare genetic variation with differences in gene activity. If a genetic variant correlates with higher or lower activity of a particular gene, this implies that the region where the variant is found plays a role in regulating the activity of the gene in question. Researchers call these regions 'expression quantitative trait loci,' or eQTL.
"Our approach revealed that long-distance regulatory connections were especially common between matching genomic neighborhoods created by genome duplication," explains Célian Diblasi, one of the lead authors of the article.
In other words, many duplicated genes appear not to have evolved entirely independently, but rather to have remained connected through shared regulatory influences that may help to preserve coordinated activity.
This observation was widespread, with a total of 230 duplicated gene pairs sharing an eQTL. Interestingly, a smaller set of 16 gene pairs showed signs of dosage compensation, where an increase or decrease in activity of one copy was matched by a compensatory shift in expression of the other copy.
How evolution preserves balance while creating novelty
These findings shed light on how genome duplication can provide new material for evolution, while preserving regulatory relationships that help maintain the dosage balance of protein-level systems where needed.
The present study implies that, even after 100 million years of divergence, some duplicated genes are still working in concert to maintain this balance.
Diblasi concludes, "Atlantic salmon will continue to provide a valuable tool for researchers studying WGD and its impact on evolution, including our own evolutionary history."
