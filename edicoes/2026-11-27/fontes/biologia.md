## Texto extraído

Protein redesign boosts DNA insertion activity and reveals how molecular machinery selects targets
Genome engineering is exciting, given its vast potential to treat and even cure diseases, but it is still a largely uncharted biomedical innovation. Scientists at St. Jude Children's Research Hospital recently uncovered the mechanisms involved in precisely integrating DNA into a target genomic location using CRISPR-associated transposases (CASTs). By studying type I-F3 CAST, the researchers unveiled structural findings that explain how this CAST family achieves successful DNA integration at precise target sites and offer insights for future genomic engineering approaches. The results were published in Nature.
The findings establish a mechanistic framework for how precise RNA-guided DNA integration could be engineered. While current genomic editing approaches have revolutionized gene therapy, CASTs could advance progress in the field and solve many existing challenges.
"There's excitement around CASTs because if you could program DNA integration where you want in a genome, that would be extremely powerful," said corresponding author Elizabeth Kellogg, Ph.D., St. Jude Department of Structural Biology.
"We are most excited for the implications in gene therapy, because CASTs can pave the way for gene-replacement tools. Instead of correcting a single mutation in a single patient the way current gene editing tools do, with CAST-based tools, we could essentially replace a defective gene with a functional copy."
Beyond structural characterization to improvement
CASTs typically encode between five and nine proteins, but there is little understanding of how these proteins work together to mediate DNA integration. The St. Jude team wanted to move beyond characterization of a CAST system toward understanding why some systems are more specific than others at genomic targeting and integration.
To better understand how the type I-F3 CAST worked, the team used advanced techniques, such as cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) and computational protein design. While cryo-EM enabled the team to visualize how the proteins interact with each other in the CAST system, computational protein design allowed them to first simulate the structure.
"When we started, we didn't have a structure. We were trying to reconstitute the full system, with all components assembled, so we began with a prediction of what it should look like," said Kellogg. "We decided to use protein design to make that assembly more stable, in the service of gaining mechanistic insight."
The study further demonstrated that protein design can be used to engineer more efficient CAST systems. "We improved the activity of the type I-F3 CAST by 100-fold without compromising its on-target specificity," said Kellogg. "That's never been done before. We are excited because it demonstrates the potential of protein design to improve CAST systems."
Solving for specificity and moving beyond structures
The study also resolved the mystery of how type I-F3 CASTs are highly specific for their target sites. "It was surprising to find these conformational changes in the proteins that basically lock them into their active state," said Kellogg. "Whenever you solve a structure, you don't know exactly what you're going to find, and this was a big surprise for us."
The researchers found the components of the CAST system behave like puzzle pieces that adapt and respond to each other, fitting together and locking into place when the correct neighbor is nearby.
The findings answer many open questions and offer insights into the principles for achieving precise RNA-guided integration. "Even though the particular CAST we are studying is site-specific, there are common principles that are conserved among the whole family," said Kellogg. "In the future, I hope we can turn more to engineering these systems based on such principles. By understanding principles, rather than just structures, we will be less restricted by what currently exists and be able to move toward building entirely new systems."
