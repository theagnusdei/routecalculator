Unusual pathogen: Fatal viral infection identified during advanced cancer immunotherapy
Modern immunotherapy may leave patients vulnerable to rare and potentially severe infections, according to a case study by researchers at the University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf (UKE) and the Leibniz Institute of Virology (LIV). The team identified an avian virus, typically harmless to humans, as the cause of a fatal brain infection in a cancer patient.
The patient, who had multiple myeloma, had undergone a stem cell transplant and was treated with the bispecific antibody teclistamab. He developed rapidly progressive encephalitis, a severe inflammation of the brain, and died despite intensive care. A highly mutated Newcastle disease virus was revealed as the cause.
Immunotherapies such as the bispecific antibody teclistamab represent a breakthrough in treating cancers like multiple myeloma, a blood cancer. By directing the body's own immune cells to specifically attack cancer cells, they offer new and highly effective treatment options for patients with this disease. However, the profound changes these therapies induce in the immune system can also increase susceptibility to rare and unusual infections.
In this unusual case, the patient had undergone a stem cell transplant and was receiving teclistamab along with other medications. He developed rapidly progressive encephalitis, a severe and life-threatening inflammation of the brain, which ultimately led to coma and death.
Despite extensive testing for known pathogens, initially no cause could be identified. The breakthrough came only through metagenomic sequencing of brain tissue: a comprehensive method that scans all genetic material in a sample without preselecting for specific microbes.
This approach revealed the presence of Newcastle disease virus, a virus typically found in poultry and wild birds. In these animals, it can cause severe respiratory and neurological disease. Human infections, by contrast, are rare and generally mild, typically causing transient conjunctivitis. In this case, the exceptionally high viral load in the brain linked the infection to the inflammation in the brain.
The research team also identified unusual changes in the viral genome: Certain genes showed an exceptionally high number of mutations, displaying a pattern that had been previously observed in chronic measles virus infections of the brain. Their study is published in the journal Acta Neuropathologica.
"The case provides the first evidence of such changes involving a paramyxovirus originating from animals, such as Newcastle disease virus. Our findings suggest that the virus was able to evolve within the immunocompromised patient and adapt to persist in nervous tissues," says Adam Grundhoff, head of the Virus Genomics Research Department at LIV.
While this case alone cannot determine how often these infections occur during advanced immunotherapies, it underscores the need to broaden diagnostic thinking in immunocompromised patients. Even normally harmless infectious agents can pose a risk in severely immunocompromised patients. This case also demonstrates the value of metagenomic sequencing in identifying the causes of otherwise unexplained infections.
"Metagenomic sequencing allows us to search for pathogens without preconceived assumptions and thereby uncover unexpected causes of infection," says Dr. Nicole Fischer, director of the Institute of Molecular Virology and Tumor Virology of the UKE and leader of the associated research group Molecular Virology and Tumor Virology at LIV. "The ability to detect such pathogens will help us better address the challenges of treating patients with weakened immune systems in the future."
The recently reported first detection of Shamonda virus in a human patient in France further underscores this challenge. Previously known primarily as an animal pathogen, the virus was identified in an immunocompromised patient who developed severe encephalitis and subsequently died. In this case, too, metagenomic sequencing played a key role in identifying the causative pathogen.
