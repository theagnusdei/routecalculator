## Texto extraído

Iron powder could store wind and solar power for winter in retired coal plants
When wind and sun are weak in winter, Europe needs power plants that can deliver energy at the push of a button—and in a climate-neutral way. So far, hydrogen and carbon capture and storage (CCS) are considered the favorites for this. Both require infrastructure that doesn't yet exist. An expert report by the Energy Economics Institute at the University of Cologne (EWI), commissioned by the Metal Energy Hubs project of TU Darmstadt and the DESERTEC Foundation, is now examining a third option: iron. TU Darmstadt and KIT have been collaborating on the Clean Circles project on iron as an energy source since 2021.
An iron energy cycle
Finely ground iron powder burns without releasing CO₂; iron oxide remains. If the oxygen is removed from this oxide using green hydrogen, the iron is "charged"—the energy from the sun and wind is then chemically stored in the powder. It can be easily stored, transported by ship or rail, and used to generate electricity in converted coal-fired power plants or in district heating plants. The iron itself is not consumed in the process, but rather cycles between being "charged" and "discharged."
Storing iron to get through the winter
The study shows that the use of iron as an energy carrier offers considerable economic and energy potential. In the European model region, annual system costs could decrease by €15 billion between 2035 and 2045 compared to a scenario without iron. For the years 2040 and 2045, the model even calculates savings of approximately €18 billion per year.
"Decarbonization and competitiveness are often seen as opposites," explains professor Christian Hasse, coordinator of the Metal Energy Hub: "The study shows that iron as an energy carrier can help to combine both aspects—and thereby save considerable costs. The significant economic potential also illustrates the contribution we as scientists can make when we consider the transfer to application from the very beginning. For me, it is fascinating to see how concrete solutions for the energy transition emerge from basic research."
Continue using existing infrastructure
A key advantage is that existing power plant infrastructure could continue to be used: By 2045, around 37 gigawatts of hard coal and lignite power plant capacity in Germany could be converted to run on iron powder; across Europe, this figure would be around 83 gigawatts. This would hardly necessitate the construction of new power plants.
At the same time, iron powder could contribute to seasonal energy storage: In summer, the modeled iron powder storage facility would be filled to around 58 terawatt-hours and discharged again in winter. This would give it a capacity comparable to that of Germany's gas storage facilities. Furthermore, the converted power plants could cover around 25% of Germany's district heating demand.
Iron also opens up new possibilities with regard to European energy supply. This energy carrier can be produced predominantly in Europe using renewable electricity, thus reducing dependence on energy imports—a significant contribution to increasing resilience. Conversely, should the expansion of renewable energies proceed more slowly than planned, imports of energy in the form of iron could serve as an additional safeguard.
Model calculation until 2045
The results are based on a model calculation for Europe up to 2045, in which electricity, hydrogen, and district heating are optimized together. The reported savings and capacities are therefore not forecasts, but model-based scenarios. Iron expands the range of available energy carriers. As an additional component of a renewable energy system, it combines the possibility of seasonal energy storage with the continued use of existing infrastructure in the transport and energy sectors.
