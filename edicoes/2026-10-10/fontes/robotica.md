Two-armed robot throws and catches balls with human-like movements
Ingrid Fadelli
Author
Humans can move their limbs very rapidly and coordinate their movements with high precision to tackle specific manual tasks. While robots have become increasingly advanced in recent years, many of them remain less agile than humans and struggle to interact with moving objects of different shapes, weights and textures.
Roboticists from the Robotics and AI Institute (RAI), headquartered in Cambridge, Massachusetts, recently developed AthenaZero, a new robotic system with two arms that could perform agile, humanlike movements. Their robot, introduced in a paper published in Science Robotics, successfully completed three baseball-inspired tasks: throwing balls, catching them and hitting them with a bat.
"Manipulation has been a challenge in robotics since the inception of our field," Andrew S. Morgan, first author of the paper, told Tech Xplore.
"Although there has been some incremental progress, we have yet to see an explosion of capabilities in this subfield as compared to locomotion or image segmentation/perception, for instance. Grounded in a mechanics-based approach, we wanted to explore what it takes to build a rigid robot that shares mass and torque (force) properties similar to those of a human."
A more agile dual-arm robotic manipulator
AthenaZero, the new robot developed by Morgan and his colleagues, consists of a central body (torso), two robotic arms with hands at their ends, motors and gears. While the structural segments of the arms are rigid, the arms' joints can yield to external forces, allowing controlled movements when the robot makes contact with an object or surface.
This design allows the robotic arms to respond to forces in their environment more effectively than many other robotic manipulators introduced in the past. Therefore, the robotic system should, at least in principle, be able to perform more agile movements and complete a wider range of manual tasks.
"Traditional robots, like those seen in factories or on cargo ships, are strong and stiff," explained Morgan. "While this quality is advantageous for picking up large objects and being very precise in their movement, these robots are inherently dangerous to work around and perform poorly when softly interacting with their environments.
"AthenaZero, on the other hand, was designed to have a larger dynamic range of force—it can be delicate when needed, but also forceful, similar to a human."
To assess their robotic manipulator, the researchers initially tested it on manual tasks inspired by baseball. They chose this sport because it requires players to perform coordinated and agile movements, sometimes involving both hands.
"The game of baseball illustrates a variety of challenging robotics topics, like soft catching and two-hand coordination," said Morgan. "Our custom actuators define our robot's mechanical properties, making it different from commercially available robots."
Improving how robots interact with real-world objects
In initial tests, AthenaZero successfully performed coordinated and agile arm movements. In the future, it could be improved and assessed on a wider range of object manipulation tasks.
"Our paper reopens the conversation about how robot manipulators (arms) should be built in order to contact the world more delicately," said Morgan.
"Over the past decade, we have seen advancements in both electric motor design and computation, which both serve as catalysts to this research thrust. AthenaZero is our first attempt at a low-inertia design paradigm, and the properties we have achieved and the results we have showcased are quite promising."
Morgan and his colleagues are now planning further studies aimed at improving their robot's movements and manipulation skills. Their hope is to ultimately create robots that can reliably complete a wider range of manual tasks in dynamic, real-world settings.
"The baseball tasks presented in our article are merely proxies for greater challenges in manipulation, where dexterous manipulation is the end goal for this large-scale project," added Morgan. "We are still interested in working on fundamental and challenging problems in the dexterous manipulation space."
