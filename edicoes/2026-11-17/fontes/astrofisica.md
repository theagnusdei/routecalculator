## Texto extraído

Dirty ice around young star Beta Pictoris suggests frequent collisions and sheds light on the system's formation
Krystal Kasal
Author
In a recent study of infrared light collected by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), a group of astronomers detected ice for the first time around the young star Beta Pictoris. Researchers had expected ice around Beta Pictoris, but previous searches had not directly detected it. The study, published in Science, found that the ice is mostly noncrystalline and mixed with carbon dioxide. The team says these new details provide clues about the early formation of the Beta Pictoris system and collisions that may still be occurring.
A young star in a dusty disk
Beta Pictoris is a roughly 20-million-year-old star about 64 light-years away, surrounded by a dusty disk containing planets and smaller bodies. While an age of 20 million years may not sound young, our own solar system is around 4.6 billion years old. So, Beta Pictoris' system is still stabilizing and forming smaller bodies within its dusty disk. So far, however, astronomers have identified three larger planets in the system, one of which was discovered earlier this year.
Earlier observations around Beta Pictoris found carbon monoxide gas, although sunlight should have destroyed it within around 50 to 300 years, a short time on the cosmic scale. The researchers say that the presence of carbon monoxide gas might mean it was recently released from collisions involving solid ice, potentially from volatile-rich comets or planetesimals. Yet, no studies had previously found evidence of the ice. For pure ice, that made sense. Models suggested that pure ice grains would disappear rapidly under the conditions in the disk, but ice mixed with other materials could survive longer.
Infrared study reveals ice
In the new study, the astronomers used JWST's near-infrared spectrograph (NIRSpec) to map the disk and analyze reflected starlight from opposite sides of the star. The new analysis directly detected signatures of water ice and carbon dioxide ice in the Beta Pictoris debris disk for the first time. The water ice was found to be overwhelmingly amorphous, meaning its molecules lack an orderly crystal structure. The team calculated that less than 7% of the ice volume could be crystalline water ice.
The spectra also showed that carbon dioxide was embedded in the water ice, with roughly one carbon dioxide molecule for every 10 water molecules. Although they say other mixtures are also possible, the carbon dioxide-to-water ratio is consistent with other spectral observations within the system.
The study authors write, "We therefore suggest that a CO₂-H₂O mixture is probably the dominant configuration of the CO₂ ice, with the CO₂ ice located in a H₂O-rich environment in the β Pictoris disk."
Old ice and ongoing collisions
The new observations of ice in Beta Pictoris resemble the ice in solar system comets and younger planet-forming disks, suggesting similar formation processes.
The study authors write, "Both crystalline and amorphous H₂O ice have been observed within the solar system. If H₂O ice condensed in amorphous form early in the star formation process, then to remain amorphous, it must have remained below ~100 K or undergone processes that disrupt the crystal lattice, such as photolysis from high-energy photons, radiolysis from galactic cosmic rays, or sputtering from energetic stellar wind ions. In the outer disk, primordial amorphous H₂O ice (AWI) might then be common owing to these effects."
This means the ice may have originated in cold interstellar material or during the earliest stages of star formation. Then some of it was likely incorporated into icy planetesimals, protecting it from crystallization. The team also says that the ice signatures were found to be broadly uniform throughout the disk, which is more likely to mean it is coming from frequent collisions among these icy bodies throughout the disk, rather than a single recent smashup.
The study authors say that impact velocities in the early debris disk stage should be insufficient to crystallize water ice. And so, continuous collisions of icy bodies are probably happening at low impact velocities, keeping the water ice in an amorphous state. The anomalous carbon monoxide found in a previous study may also be explained by this process. Frequent collisions are likely supplying new carbon monoxide as it gets constantly destroyed by the star's light.
The study may also offer context for understanding our own solar system's icy ingredients. In future studies, comparisons across debris disks in systems of different ages could reveal how ice changes as planetary systems mature.
