Crigler-Najjar syndrome: Gene therapy maintains its efficacy four years after treatment
The New England Journal of Medicine has published a study on the long-term efficacy of AAV8-mediated gene therapy (GNT0003) in patients with Crigler-Najjar syndrome, an extremely rare congenital liver disorder affecting approximately 500 people in Europe. It is caused by a deficiency of UGT1A1, an enzyme required for bilirubin metabolism. The accumulation of this substance can lead to irreversible neurological damage and, in the most severe cases, death.
To date, patients have relied on blue-light phototherapy to control their bilirubin levels. Liver transplantation is the only definitive curative treatment option, but it carries a risk of complications. GNT 0003 is a gene therapy aiming to restore UGT1A1 expression in the liver and correct bilirubin levels in patients.
Benefits persist four years after treatment
Lorenzo D'Antiga, director of the pediatrics department at Papa Giovanni XXIII Hospital, and his co-authors documented the persistence of treatment benefits four years after administration in the three patients who received the therapeutic dose at the hospital in Bergamo, Italy. Two of them were able to discontinue phototherapy, while the third maintained a partial response, requiring lower exposure to phototherapy. In all three patients, bilirubin levels remained significantly below the threshold associated with the risk of neurotoxicity.
The investigators also observed a sustained normalization of levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), a liver enzyme that is a key marker of liver health and function, in the three patients treated at the highest dose, an effect that had never been achieved with phototherapy alone.
One of the patients also successfully completed a pregnancy without complications following gene therapy and gave birth to a healthy baby.
Testing efficacy in a larger population
This latest publication follows the results of the Phase I–II CureCN trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2023. Those results demonstrated the safety of the treatment and its ability to correct the disease, enabling patients who received the therapeutic dose to discontinue phototherapy.
The pivotal part of the trial, which began in January 2023, aims to confirm the observed effect in a larger patient population, including children age 10 and older, as this is the age at which the liver reaches maturity.
Potential beyond Crigler-Najjar syndrome
"The results of this clinical trial open a new path toward the correction of genetic liver diseases that affect both children and adults and would otherwise be incurable," says D'Antiga, director of the pediatrics department at Papa Giovanni XXIII Hospital and principal investigator of the study.
"Until now, adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector gene therapy has shown clinical efficacy in patients with hemophilia A and B. There is now also a clear signal of its efficacy in Crigler–Najjar syndrome. Offering real hope of a cure to patients with severe rare diseases is the greatest reward for physicians and researchers in this field.
"At Papa Giovanni XXIII Hospital in Bergamo, we continue our commitment to clinical research with passion and dedication, bringing to the bedside innovative therapies that may one day be available to all patients with diseases that currently lack a definitive treatment."
"These four-year follow-up results confirm the potential of GNT0003 to change the management of Crigler-Najjar syndrome in the long term. They confirm not only the safety of our approach but, above all, its potential for sustained efficacy over time," says Giuseppe Ronzitti, from Généthon's Immunology and Gene Therapy for Liver Diseases team.
"The teams at Genethon have devoted considerable effort to this project, from initial design of the treatment to clinical trial, and we are very happy with these results, which pave the way for the treatment of other metabolic diseases. This study and those to come also reflect our long-term commitment to finding therapeutic solutions for rare and ultra-rare diseases."
