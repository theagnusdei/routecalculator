## Texto extraído

In a nutshell: NASA's Voyager 1 has become the first human-made object to travel one light-day from Earth. The milestone occurred on November 18 at 5:16:07 a.m. Eastern time, roughly nine years before its twin spacecraft, Voyager 2, will achieve the same feat.
One "day" might not sound like a lot of time or distance, and in the vast emptiness of space, where everything is so far apart, it really isn't. Rather than using familiar measurements such as miles or kilometers, distances in space are often described in terms of how light travels.
Light moves through space at approximately 186,000 miles per second, and nothing travels faster. The Moon, for reference, is roughly 238,855 miles from Earth on average, and it takes light about 1.3 seconds to travel that distance. At 93 million miles away, sunlight takes about eight minutes and 20 seconds to reach our planet. Astronomers also use the latter, called an astronomical unit (or AU for short), as a unit of length in space.
As you might have guessed, a light-day is the distance light travels in 24 hours: 16,094,799,096 miles (25,902,068,356 kilometers), to be exact. A light-year, by comparison, is roughly 5.88 trillion miles.
NASA launched the two Voyager probes in 1977 to study planets in the outskirts of our solar system, including gas giants Jupiter and Saturn. Voyager 1, which lifted off on September 5, completed its planetary objectives and entered into an extended mission phase in 1980. Voyager 2 launched a couple of weeks earlier on August 20.
Voyager 1 eventually became the first human-made object to reach interstellar space in 2012, with Voyager 2 following suit in 2018. To date, these are the only two spacecraft to have ever achieved this milestone.
To reiterate, both probes have been careening through space since the mid-70s, and only just now has one made it a single light-day from Earth. And to think, some objects in the universe are many billions of light-years away. That truly is absolutely mind-blowing.
