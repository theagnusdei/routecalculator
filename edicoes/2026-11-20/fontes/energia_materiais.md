## Texto extraído

Harvesting electricity from prickly pear cacti
In a greenhouse at the University of California, Santa Cruz, electricity gently hums, but it isn't coming from a lightbulb or socket. Instead, the voltage is being produced from the stems of prickly pear cacti, which researchers converted into living solar panels.
A team of scientists developed a technique to harvest small amounts of power from cacti that takes advantage of the plants' specialized, water-preserving approach to photosynthesis. They envision that this green renewable electricity source could power sensors on farms that measure crop conditions amid increasing climate change–related pressures.
A new study published in the journal Plants, People, Planet demonstrates the team's approach to capturing energy from prickly pear cacti and shows how environmental conditions affect cactus voltage strength.
Undergraduate researchers from three academic divisions at UC Santa Cruz contributed to this project, enabling the students to design climate solutions with an approach that felt true to the campus' spirit.
"The undergraduate students told me they liked this project so much because it felt whimsical, and like a lighthearted approach to something that is so serious," said Charlie Chesney, an environmental studies Ph.D. alumna who led the project. "It was a fun way to explore climate solutions that put creativity first."
Electricity from acid
Environmental studies professor Michael Loik, who has been studying cactus physiology and ecology for around 40 years, spearheaded the project with Chesney.
"I've been obsessed with the plant since forever, it seems," Loik said.
To transform cacti into power generators, the researchers take advantage of their specialized method for photosynthesis, called crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM). Prickly pear cacti and other plants such as aloe, pineapple and dragon fruit use CAM photosynthesis to preserve water for survival in hot, dry conditions.
CAM plants process carbon dioxide into sugar over the course of 24 hours to take advantage of cooler temperatures during the night, while all other plants only photosynthesize while the sun is out.
Other plants open small pores in their leaves during the day to take in carbon dioxide (CO₂) and use sunlight to convert it to sugar—but opening up the pores lets water escape. To preserve precious water, CAM plants only take in CO₂ at night, converting it to a chemical called malic acid.
When the sun comes up and hits the cactus, photosynthesis begins to break down the malic acid, but only on the side of the plant facing the light. That's because CAM plants have thick, opaque stems—another technique to conserve water—which only perform photosynthesis in the outer two millimeters of each side.
This difference in acidity between the sunny and shady sides of the plant causes a pH concentration gradient, which creates a voltage that the researchers harvested.
Turning plants into solar panels
Loik's extensive knowledge of prickly pear cacti led him to predict that electricity production would be possible, but he, Chesney and the team set out to prove and measure it with cacti in plots at the UCSC Farm, greenhouses and laboratories.
Chesney assembled a large team of undergraduate students from the Engineering, Sciences, and Social Sciences divisions at UC Santa Cruz, enabling each student to contribute their knowledge and interests to the project. Now, seven of those former undergraduate researchers are published study authors.
"This was such a cool demonstration of being a really, truly interdisciplinary project, and bringing together so many different undergrads who had different focuses," Chesney said. "It was just so nice to have 10 students in a room from all across campus working together on a single project. When we're looking at climate solutions, that's what we need."
After years of experimentation, the team found their cactus solar panels could produce a voltage of up to half a volt, which they harvest via a wired electrode inserted into the plant. The team also pursued methods to increase the voltage by adding multiple wires to harvest energy and connecting cactus stems together. By linking together eight cactus stems, they generated a whopping 4.5 volts of electricity—by comparison, a standard AA battery generates 1.5 volts.
Now that Chesney has graduated, electrical and computer engineering assistant professor Colleen Josephson and her Ph.D. student John Madden are carrying forward the project to further enhance the electrical signals produced from the plants.
On-farm energy sources
The team envisions that electricity harvested from the prickly pear cacti could be used to charge low-power agricultural monitoring sensors, which are increasingly in demand due to rising pressures on crops from climate change. By planting prickly pear cacti alongside other crops, farmers would have a renewable power source for sensors to monitor field conditions.
Or, the electricity harvesting could serve to monitor the health of the plant, as the plant is commonly grown for its edible stems (nopales) and fruits (tunas). The timing and magnitude of the voltage, a direct reflection of the cacti's photosynthesis, offer a window into the plant's health and that of the surrounding environment.
"There are many commercial applications of cacti for monitoring their health using a low-power system like ours, that is just operating off on the side and is self-powered," Loik said. "This could be a really interesting avenue to pursue. It could be connected to a plant that acts as a sentinel, measuring conditions and sending it back to the base station at the farmer's office."
The researchers hypothesize that their technique could work with other CAM plants like agave or pineapple, although some plants may be more sensitive to the wires needed to harvest electricity.
Impact of environmental factors
The team wanted to investigate the impact of environmental conditions on voltage production for on-farm cactus sensors. They found that the voltage is much higher in the summer when there's lots of sunlight versus during the winter.
"Just how closely the voltage aligned with the sunlight conditions was really surprising," Chesney said. "Comparing winter to summer voltage, the voltage really follows the same pattern of the amount of sunlight throughout the day."
However, too much sunlight can also be a problem.
"If it's too high, then the cactus will start trying to protect itself from sunburn, which will also decrease the voltage," Chesney said. "There's a balance, a midrange of light that causes the voltage to be higher."
They also looked into the effect of drought, a common challenge for cacti. After 15 months without water, not only were the cacti still alive and photosynthesizing, but sometimes the voltage in the drought-stressed stems was actually higher than in the well-watered stems.
"This was a really surprising result, but also showed how resilient a cactus is—it could still produce electricity even when it seems, to us, like it's near death," Chesney said.
Led by undergraduate Erin Langness, the team also investigated the impact of nitrogen, which is a common ingredient in fertilizers because it boosts photosynthesis. Adding nitrogen to the prickly pear cacti boosted the plants' supply of malic acid, which meant photosynthesis and voltage production lasted later into the day.
