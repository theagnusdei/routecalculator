Prostate cancer drug shows promise in new clinical trial
In a recent Yale-led clinical trial, a new type of prostate cancer drug showed promising results in reducing tumor activity and pain for patients with metastatic prostate cancer.
"This is a first-in-class drug for prostate cancer," says Daniel Petrylak, professor of medicine (oncology and hematology) at Yale School of Medicine and senior author of the study, published Oct. 9 in the Journal of Clinical Oncology.
Patients in the phase 1/phase 2 clinical trial were diagnosed and previously treated for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), an incurable, lethal form of the disease. Oncologists typically treat prostate cancer by starving it of testosterone, which halts growth for a time. But with mCRPC, the cancer continues to grow even with limited testosterone.
"More than 35,000 men will die from mCRPC in the United States this year," says Petrylak, a member of Yale Cancer Center. "It's the most advanced form of prostate cancer."
Bavdegalutamide is the first PROTAC androgen receptor degrader to be clinically tested. PROTACs are a new class of cancer drug that use proteolysis, the natural process wherein a cell breaks down proteins to recycle the materials, to degrade cancers. PROTACs were pioneered by Yale researcher Craig Crews, including the first PROTAC approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for use in breast cancer.
Bavdegalutamide was designed to treat mCRPC by bypassing its resistance to other hormone therapies. "Bavdegalutamide takes your own natural protein degradation system, and it accelerates the process for the androgen receptor. The drug degrades the receptors for testosterone, which control prostate cancer growth," says Petrylak.
In phase 1 of the trial, the researchers found that a 420-milligram daily dose was safe for patients who had already been treated for mCRPC. In phase 2, they evaluated the drug's effectiveness. The team of researchers and clinicians measured prostate-specific antigen, which is the hallmark of mCRPC, before and after administering the drug.
"There was a decrease in prostate-specific antigen. We also saw regressions in soft tissue lesions and improvements in pain," says Petrylak. "These are the parameters that indicate a positive response."
The research collaborators also studied the effect that bavdegalutamide had on prostate cancers with different mutations. In cancers with what are known as T878 and/or H875 mutations of androgen receptors, bavdegalutamide led to a greater reduction of prostate-specific antigen. The study's authors point to this result as an indicator of PROTAC drugs being used as precision medicine.
The researchers and clinician collaborators are currently testing a second PROTAC, called ARV 766, in phase 1 and phase 2 clinical trials. This drug is active against a broader spectrum of androgen receptor mutations than ARV-110.
