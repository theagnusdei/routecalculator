Anode-free sodium batteries last longer thanks to an electrolyte that forms its own shield
Ingrid Fadelli
Author
Sodium batteries, which store and release electrical energy by moving sodium ions between their two electrodes, could be a more affordable alternative to lithium-based batteries. This is because sodium is highly abundant on Earth and can be sourced from widely available raw materials.
Some energy researchers have also been exploring sodium batteries that are assembled without a sodium-metal anode, known as anode-free sodium batteries. In these batteries, sodium ions originating from the cathode (i.e., the positive electrode while the battery is discharging) gain electrons and deposit as sodium metal onto a thin metal current collector during charging, forming the anode.
This anode-free design could further reduce manufacturing costs, while also increasing the energy that sodium batteries can store relative to their weight. Despite their potential, anode-free sodium batteries have a limited supply of sodium, and the irreversible loss of sodium as they are charged and discharged can reduce their capacity and cycle life.
Researchers at Xi'an Jiaotong University, the Chinese Academy of Sciences and other institutions recently designed a new electrolyte for anode-free sodium batteries that incorporates the solvent 1,3-dioxane (DOX). The electrolyte, presented in a paper published in Nature Energy, was found to extend the lifespan of anode-free pouch cells, slowing down their loss of capacity.
"Our group began working on sodium-ion batteries in 2022, initially focusing on electrolyte design for conventional batteries using hard-carbon anodes," Weijiang Xue, senior author of the paper, told Tech Xplore.
"Through discussions with industry, we realized that the cost of hard carbon was a major obstacle to commercialization. Drawing on our previous experience with anode-free lithium-metal batteries, we saw an opportunity to eliminate the hard-carbon anode entirely."
An electrolyte that limits sodium loss
The recent study by Xue and his colleagues builds on earlier works highlighting the differences between lithium and sodium metals. Sodium is typically softer than lithium and can slowly change shape (i.e., creep) under pressure at room temperature.
Due to these characteristics, the thin protective layer separating sodium electrodes from electrolytes might benefit from different mechanical properties than those of the interface in lithium batteries. The researchers thus set out to design an electrolyte that could enable the formation of a better-suited interphase in sodium batteries.
"Rather than simply pursuing the classic inorganic-rich protective layers in lithium-metal batteries, we aimed to minimize continuous electrolyte decomposition through rational molecular design," said Xue. "This ultimately led us to explore controlled interfacial oligomerization as a strategy for achieving long-lasting anode-free sodium batteries."
The team initially examined various cyclic ethers, chemical compounds containing a ring of connected atoms that includes an oxygen atom bonded to two carbon atoms. They studied ethers with rings made up of five or six atoms, to determine how the rings' structure and internal strain influenced reactions at the sodium-metal surface.
"We selected DOX because its weak coordination with sodium ions facilitates reversible sodium deposition, while its controlled oligomerization forms a thin, protective interphase that suppresses further electrolyte decomposition," explained Xue.
After designing their electrolyte, the researchers incorporated it in anode-free sodium pouch cells. They measured these cells' performance at different charging/discharging rates, also measuring how much capacity the cells retained at different temperatures and after 1,400 charge-discharge cycles.
"We combined theoretical calculations, electrochemical measurements and advanced characterization techniques to understand these processes, particularly the molecular composition of the interphase," said Xue. "Importantly, rather than relying solely on laboratory coin cells, we extensively tested the electrolytes in 0.3-Ah anode-free pouch cells, providing a more practical and scalable assessment of their cycling stability."
Toward longer-lasting anode-free sodium batteries
The team's newly designed electrolyte was found to extend the cycle life of anode-free sodium pouch cells beyond 1,000 cycles. With this electrolyte, pouch cells retained approximately 80% of their initial capacity after 1,400 charge-discharge cycles at 0.25 C/1 C.
"Equally important is the molecular-level identification of oligomeric species in the protective interphase," said Xue. "This finding broadens the conventional understanding of interphase design, showing that stable interfaces for Na-metal do not necessarily require inorganic-rich layers derived from salt decomposition. We believe this opens new possibilities for designing electrolytes for next-generation metal batteries."
The recent efforts by Xue and his colleagues could contribute to the advancement and future commercialization of anode-free sodium batteries. Meanwhile, the researchers are planning more research aimed at designing new electrolytes for anode-free sodium batteries. For instance, they would like to introduce promising solid-state electrolytes and electrolytes that are compatible with high-voltage layered-oxide cathodes.
"Our long-term goal is to achieve energy densities comparable to those of today's high-energy lithium-ion batteries using nickel-rich cathodes, while maintaining more than 1,000 cycles of stable operation," added Xue. "We hope these advances will bring low-cost, high-energy-density sodium batteries closer to practical applications."
