## Texto extraído

JWST finds a possible black hole that grew faster than its host galaxy in the early universe
Shreejaya Karantha
Author
How the first massive black holes formed in the universe remains an open question in astronomy. Black holes weighing about a billion solar masses within the first billion years of the universe require extraordinary origin stories.
Now, a new multiwavelength study of a potential black hole may offer a clue. Combining spectroscopy from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) with Hubble imaging and archival data from the Very Large Telescope's MUSE instrument, astronomers identified this black hole in MARTA 3887, a clumpy, metal-poor galaxy as seen about 12 billion years ago. This black hole appears to have a mass of about 3.3 million suns and sits in a galaxy containing roughly 145 million solar masses in stars. The discovery paper, posted to the arXiv preprint server, explains why this is unusual.
Overmassive black holes
Many recent studies have revealed early black holes that have a different relationship with their host galaxies, compared to what's seen in the nearby universe. Early black holes are too heavy compared to their host galaxies' stellar masses. This suggests the black holes in the early universe may grow quicker than the stars.
Researchers point out another factor that may affect the 'overmassive black holes' sample. Surveys can only detect black holes that are bright or fast-moving enough, so the samples have the heaviest ones for a given galaxy mass. Those giant black holes are only the tip of the iceberg. To really understand how black holes form and grow, astronomers need to look for the smaller, more typical ones too. But these are also hard to detect.
The signals from supernovae exploding into dense gas, shocks, and outflows can mimic faint black holes. In young, metal-poor galaxies, the spectral line ratios used to differentiate black hole-powered gas from star-powered gas may overlap, and extreme stars can mimic an active galactic nucleus (AGN), a galaxy powered by a massive black hole.
'Outpaced' growth
In the new study, Mirko Curti of the INAF—Astrophysics and Space Science Observatory of Bologna and colleagues used many lines of evidence rather than one to ask whether the object at the center of MARTA 3887 is an overmassive black hole.
Using the spectroscopic observations from JWST, they detected a wide hydrogen-alpha line, which revealed that the gas is moving at about 1,270 kilometers per second (790 miles per second). The signal appeared only in hydrogen, not in other kinds of lines such as those from oxygen that come from calmer gas. This pointed to a black hole rather than an ordinary outflow.
Next, they estimated the black hole's mass: roughly 3.3 million times the sun's mass. This mass estimate places it among the lower-mass black holes found by JWST. The galaxy, on the other hand, has about 145 million solar masses of stars, so the black hole is roughly 2.3% of the stellar mass.
Researchers note that this relation is "approximately 2 dex above the local relation," which means it is about 100 times higher than the relationship extrapolated from nearby galaxies. This suggests that "black-hole growth has outpaced the assembly of the observed stellar component," the team writes in the paper.
The galaxy itself is compact and clumpy and is low in heavy elements, about 10% of the sun's level, which is typical of a young galaxy. It is forming stars fast, roughly five to nine solar masses of new stars per year, mostly in a recent burst.
Growth tracking
The researchers then explored how the 'outpacing' could have happened. They built simple growth trackers assuming one black hole grows steadily from a seed, with no mergers, at a constant feeding rate. Each track was set to end at the measured mass at the time they observed the galaxy.
They found that if black hole growth began very early, when the universe was about 0.1 billion years old, the seeds with masses of 100, 10,000, and 100,000 suns would need to feed at about 34%, 19%, and 11% of the maximum rate. If growth began only with the recent star-formation burst, a 100-solar-mass seed would need to feed at about 570% of the maximum rate, which is extreme. This means a small black-hole seed that began its growth later would require strongly super-Eddington growth, while a more massive initial seed could grow more slowly.
The team is careful to note that this galaxy 'possibly' hosts a black hole. An interacting supernova or a compact and thick outflow could still mimic the signal, though these explanations are less likely. They cannot rule out these scenarios from data gathered during a single observation.
A possible 'leaker'
The galaxy may also be leaking ultraviolet light. Some of the high-energy ultraviolet photons from early galaxies had to escape into space to help clear the neutral hydrogen fog of the early universe during the cosmic reionization period. Several signs suggest MARTA 3887 has porous gas through which such light could escape. About 44% of the Lyman-alpha light produced by the galaxy escapes, which is a high fraction. Because they cannot yet measure the escape fraction of the ultraviolet photons, the team calls MARTA 3887 an indirect "leaker candidate."
Overall, this black hole candidate extends the individually detected population of actively feeding black holes into the faint, metal-poor, low-mass host regime, and suggests that its growth may have outpaced the buildup of its stars.
