## Texto extraído

Model shows factories and employees can profit from idle car batteries at work
Researchers from Skoltech—a VEB.RF Group institution—have developed an optimization framework to help factories compare strategies for using onsite solar power and the electric vehicles of employees to reduce electricity costs. Evaluated in a Moscow case study, the approach accounts for industrial electricity billing rules, operating decisions and drivers' willingness to participate, helping businesses assess options before investing.
The study was published in Applied Energy.
As the share of renewables in the global electricity mix is projected to keep growing, finding enough land for solar and wind farms and other generation facilities is becoming increasingly difficult. This challenge is compounded by the need for infrastructure upgrades where existing transmission lines cannot carry the additional output.
Generating electricity close to where it is consumed could provide part of the solution by cutting the need for long-distance transmission and making use of existing buildings or developed sites. Factories are prime candidates: Their large electricity demand can absorb on-site generation, and their roofs and industrial grounds mitigate the need for new land.
Businesses considering this route need to work out how to combine available resources into a financially worthwhile project. Cars parked at work sit idle for hours.
With bidirectional chargers, which allow electricity to flow into and out of car batteries, employees' electric vehicles could supply part of a factory's needs. Known as vehicle-to-grid technology, this approach can add flexibility to a factory's energy use. Unlike stationary storage, however, cars arrive and leave with their owners, who need enough charge at the end of the day for making their trips and who may opt out entirely.
Factory owners can manage when cars charge and whether participating vehicles may supply power back to the workplace. Each arrangement involves different operating decisions and financial considerations. Add solar generation into the mix, and the picture changes further.
For a case study, the researchers looked at the Russian market, where industrial consumers pay for electricity at hourly prices and for capacity linked to demand during particular peak hours. Solar generation and flexible vehicle charging can therefore reduce different parts of the electricity bill.
The Skoltech team identified a lack of long-term investment assessments that would account for solar power, workplace vehicle-to-grid supply, driver participation and Russian billing rules. The framework proposed in the study links driver participation, charging prices, compensation for supplying electricity and car battery charge levels. This means that the incentives offered determine how much flexibility is available: Not every connected car is automatically counted as available.
For a test period of 10 years, the researchers compared six supply scenarios: grid-only supply, solar panels alone, vehicle-to-grid alone, solar with managed charging, and combined solar and vehicle-to-grid under fixed and behavioral participation assumptions.
Under an unchanging vehicle fleet and solar infrastructure, the model jointly optimized operating schedules, charging prices and driver compensation within battery and connectivity limits, then assessed the resulting financial returns.
Distinct economic opportunities emerged depending on the scenario: While solar generation reduced electricity purchases, coordinating vehicle charging and discharging created additional value under the case-study conditions. Managed charging provided benefits without requiring battery discharge, and bidirectional operation offered further flexibility.
Accounting for driver participation produced more conservative financial estimates than assuming every technically available car participates. In terms of capital efficiency, vehicle-to-grid solutions alone are expected to recoup their investment faster than when paired with solar cells, even though the latter approach yields greater long-term returns.
"Employees' electric cars create an opportunity to use the same battery for personal transport and workplace energy flexibility. We factor drivers' willingness to participate into the coordination of solar power, charging and discharging. This helps businesses compare operating arrangements and assess whether the additional flexibility justifies the investment," said the study's lead author, Ahmed Eltwam, a Ph.D. student in Skoltech's Engineering Systems program.
"A systematic assessment of project viability could encourage more investment in onsite solar power and workplace vehicle-to-grid services. Businesses could lower electricity costs, and employees could be paid for the flexibility their vehicles provide. At a broader scale, deployment coordinated with grid needs could support national renewable energy targets while avoiding some of the costly network upgrades," said the study's principal investigator, associate professor of the practice Marina Dolmatova of Skoltech Energy.
The findings are based on simulations with estimated market and operating inputs. Participation assumptions were informed by published research rather than a workplace survey. Although evaluated for Moscow, the framework can be adapted to local electricity prices, billing rules, demand, weather and vehicle availability.
Financial outcomes remain specific to the analyzed conditions, including the alignment of operation with capacity-charge hours. Testing and calibrating the model against real workplace charging behavior and operating data is a priority for future research.
