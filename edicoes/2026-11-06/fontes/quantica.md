What happens when you zip a 10-year-old into a full clean room suit and walk him into one of the most advanced quantum labs in Europe?
In the newest episode of The Quantum Kid, Kai gets to find out. He spent a day at Microsoft’s quantum lab in Lyngby, just outside Copenhagen. It’s Microsoft’s largest quantum site, and the place where the heart of its Majorana chips is made. The question he went to answer is a big one: how do you build a qubit that protects itself from errors?
A particle, a mystery and a qubit
The story starts in 1937, when Italian physicist Ettore Majorana predicted a particle that is its own antiparticle. A year later, he boarded a ferry from Palermo to Naples and vanished. What happened to him is still a mystery.
Nearly a century on, his name is at the centre of Microsoft’s quantum computing strategy. Microsoft is trying to build topological qubits using Majorana zero modes: exotic quantum states that should appear at the ends of special superconducting nanowires.
The idea is that a topological qubit stores quantum information spread out across the wire, rather than in one spot. That should make it much harder for a small disturbance to destroy it. Most quantum computers today fight a constant battle against noise and will rely on error correction to catch mistakes after they happen. Microsoft wants qubits that are far less prone to errors in the first place.
Three labs, one chip
Kai’s guide was Lauri Sainiemi, Vice President of Fabrication at Microsoft Quantum. He took Kai through every stage of making a Majorana chip.
The materials lab. It all starts with molecular beam epitaxy machines, which grow the chip’s base material one layer of atoms at a time inside an ultra-high vacuum. They combine a semiconductor with a superconductor to build the layered material Microsoft calls a topoconductor.
The clean room. Next, in full overalls, Kai saw how that material is patterned into tiny nanowire devices on the chip. Since the lab’s expansion in 2025, Lyngby can fabricate the full core of Microsoft’s Majorana chips.
The cryostat lab. Finally, the finished chips go into the gold “chandelier” fridges. These cool them to almost absolute zero, colder than outer space, which is where the quantum effects the qubits rely on appear and where the team tests whether the devices work.

Where Microsoft’s bet stands
The visit comes at an important moment for Microsoft’s topological programme. In June 2026, the company unveiled Majorana 2, its second-generation chip. Microsoft says it improves qubit reliability 1,000-fold over Majorana 1, with parity lifetimes averaging around 20 seconds, thanks to a new lead-based material stack. It has also brought forward its target for a scalable quantum computer to 2029.
It remains one of the boldest and most closely watched approaches in the field, and physicists continue to debate how to confirm that the devices are truly topological. That is exactly what makes a look inside the lab so interesting: this is the place where the claims are turned into hardware, one layer of atoms at a time.
Explained like you’re 10
Most people will never set foot in a quantum fabrication lab. Through Kai’s questions, they can see how it works: why the materials have to be perfect, why the chips need to be so cold, and why a physicist’s nearly 90-year-old prediction matters to one of the world’s biggest tech companies.
