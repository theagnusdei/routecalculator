## Texto extraído

Two new studies point to advances in personalized lung cancer treatment
Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death in the United States, but for a growing number of patients, the outlook is improving. As Lung Cancer Awareness Month draws attention to the disease and advances in care, Marina Garassino, M.D., a thoracic oncologist at the University of Chicago Medicine, is helping push the field forward.
She has coauthored two new studies focused on making lung cancer treatment more personalized and effective. Both were published.
One paper informs when some patients should receive different types of treatment, while the other uses artificial intelligence to determine which patients may benefit most from a promising new drug.
Immunotherapy before lung cancer surgery improves five-year outcomes
A new five-year analysis of the international KEYNOTE-671 clinical trial, published in Annals of Oncology, finds that some patients with early-stage, operable non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) may have better outcomes when they receive chemotherapy and immunotherapy before surgery rather than receiving chemotherapy alone.
"Doctors' and patients' first instinct is to cut the tumor out immediately, which is understandable," said Garassino, a member of the trial's steering committee and senior author of the paper. "But our research demonstrates that in some cases, taking the time for chemotherapy and immunotherapy can increase the number of patients who are cured and improve overall survival with fewer recurrences."
The trial enrolled nearly 800 patients with resectable stage II or III NSCLC. Half received chemotherapy plus the immunotherapy drug pembrolizumab before surgery and continued pembrolizumab afterward. The other half received chemotherapy plus a placebo.
The five-year results show a significant difference between the groups. Nearly half (49.9%) of patients who received pembrolizumab were alive without their cancer returning or progressing five years later, nearly double the 26.5% of patients in the placebo group. Overall survival was also higher in the pembrolizumab group: 64.6%, compared with 53.6% in the placebo group.
Adding pembrolizumab did not worsen patients' reported quality of life. Side effects were consistent with what doctors would expect from the treatments.
The approach is now approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and has become the standard of care. Future studies will expand the patient population beyond those with clearly resectable tumors.
Garassino's message to patients with NSCLC: "Patience is worthwhile. Undergoing chemotherapy and immunotherapy before your surgery can change your life."
AI helps predict which lung cancer patients may benefit from a new drug
A second study, published in Nature Medicine with Garassino as first author, explores how artificial intelligence could help doctors identify which patients with NSCLC are most likely to benefit from a promising new treatment.
The drug, datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd), is designed to deliver powerful chemotherapy directly inside cancer cells. But not all eligible patients with lung cancer respond to it.
"We want to give the right drug to the right patients and spare anyone who is unlikely to benefit from possible side effects," Garassino said.
Dato-DXd targets a protein called TROP2 on the surface of cancer cells. However, about 90% of NSCLC tumors have TROP2, so the protein's presence or quantity alone isn't a reliable predictor of efficacy.
Garassino and her research collaborators suspected the answer lay in the protein's specific location inside the cell. They trained AI models to analyze digitized biopsy slides and calculate how TROP2 is distributed on the surface and inside thousands of cells.
"The protein concentration is visible in the cell cytoplasm, but the human eye is not able to give a quantitative score," Garassino said.
The analysis revealed that tumors with more TROP2 inside their cells were more likely to respond to Dato-DXd. Researchers suspect this pattern reflects cancer cells actively pulling the protein inward, potentially bringing the drug into the cells with it.
In a Phase III clinical trial, patients who tested positive for the AI-derived biomarker and received Dato-DXd lived a median of 17 months, compared with 10.8 months for those who tested negative.
Among patients who received standard chemotherapy instead, biomarker status did not affect survival. This suggests the test may help identify patients who are more likely to benefit specifically from Dato-DXd.
"It's an early example of how a good use of artificial intelligence and machine learning is able to improve precision medicine in clinical trials," Garassino said.
