## Texto extraído

Scientists pinpoint DNA copying 'traffic jams' impacting genome stability
Creating an accurate copy of the genome—the entire length of DNA in a cell—is the fundamental step before cells can divide, the basis on which all life sits. Errors in that duplication process—including replication pauses—lead to natural genetic changes that underpin evolution, but also give rise to diseases such as cancer.
However, identifying the frequency and impact of these events is inherently challenging due to their low frequency and delocalized nature across the genome.
Researchers in the Nieduszynski Group at Earlham Institute have now mapped locations in budding yeast where the copying machinery pauses, or stops altogether, in much greater detail. The paper "Single-molecule landscape of DNA replication pausing" is published in Nature Communications.
Head-on conflicts increase replication pauses
Using a single-molecule technology that labels newly made DNA with a chemical tag called BrdU, researchers could see individual replication events and determine where pauses occurred, which direction the replication machinery (replisome) was traveling, which DNA strand it was copying, roughly how long it paused, and whether replication resumed.
The study found that pauses are particularly prevalent in transcribed regions (around 20%–30% more problematic) and are particularly enhanced when transcription-replication conflicts are head-on.
While these conflicts have been observed before, previous techniques largely analyzed events at a population-scale level and therefore were unable to fully examine the scale of the problem or how frequently these events occurred.
Capturing rare events molecule by molecule
By working at the single-molecule level, the researchers can instead study individual replication events, as well as rarer events that would be difficult to detect using population-level approaches.
Dr. Sathish Thiyagarajan, postdoctoral researcher in the Nieduszynski Group, said, "We can see replication-pausing hotspots in much greater detail. More importantly, we now have a handle on how many replication events encounter problems. Because we can capture individual events, we can zoom in on particular genomic regions and see what's happening even when the event is relatively rare.
"So, if someone is interested in a particular gene and knows that problems in replication at that gene have downstream effects, this technique gives them a way to zoom in on that region and capture individual replication events."
The research builds on an earlier study by the Nieduszynski Group, which used the same single-molecule technology to map initiation sites—where the double-stranded helix teases apart to begin copying—finding that these initiation sites, like pausing, are dispersed across the genome, with around 80% of sites never identified before.
"Together with our earlier study, we can now track on a single DNA molecule where replication initiates, the obstacles it encounters, and the consequences for genome stability and gene regulation," adds Thiyagarajan.
Pause hotspots may reshape gene activity
The study found specific hotspots where parts of the copying machine come apart during a pause, which the researchers believe could act as a molecular switch, altering how that region of the genome is packaged and potentially having lasting effects on which genes are turned on or off in a cell.
At two particular sites, they found locations where the leading strand encountered an obstacle, while replication on the lagging strand continued.
Dr. Conrad Nieduszynski, group leader, said, "This work provides a powerful new way to identify difficult-to-replicate regions across genomes. These regions are likely to be hotspots for genetic and epigenetic change, with consequences that can range from microbial adaptation and biofilm formation to genome instability and disease in more complex organisms."
"One thing that we were pleased with was how well the technique could distinguish between replication on the leading and lagging strands. We knew the technique had the potential to distinguish between the two types of replication, but I thought we would need much more data to see meaningful differences. Instead, we saw many more leading-strand pauses than lagging-strand pauses at particular locations," said Thiyagarajan.
By making rare replication pauses easier to detect and quantify, the researchers hope to help scientists better understand how and why replication problems arise and how they may contribute to human genetic disorders.
