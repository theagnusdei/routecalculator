## Texto extraído

Proteins with ancient viral roots guard genetic switches left by mobile DNA
Much of our genome contains mobile pieces or "transposable elements": pieces of DNA that spread and accumulated over millions of years, some originating from ancient viruses. Although many are no longer mobile, their sequences can influence when nearby genes switch on or off.
The genome's internal fossils
As they evolved, cells have even repurposed some of these sequences to develop new ways of regulating genes. But this creates a challenge.
Transposable elements can provide useful new genetic "switches," but if their activity becomes uncontrolled, it can affect gene regulation and hurt or even destroy the cell. So cells have evolved ways to keep transposable elements in check.
One well-studied system uses the so-called "KRAB zinc-finger proteins" (KZFPs). These recruit machinery that adds chemical marks to the proteins that package DNA in the cell (called histones), helping lock nearby DNA regions into a closed, inactive state.
However, the number of transposable elements is vast and only a small fraction of them are bound by KZFPs. A team from the Laboratory of virology and genetics at EPFL, led by Didier Trono, with his postdoc Wayo Matsushima, has discovered another family of controlling proteins, called "SCAN zinc-finger proteins" (SZFPs). They have now shown that SZFPs commonly target regulatory sequences derived from transposable elements and keep them inactive through a different mechanism.
The study, published in Nature Genetics, points to a new way in which cells protect their existing gene activity while also allowing the genome to acquire new regulatory sequences over evolutionary time.
A protein family born from ancient viruses
The researchers traced the SZFP family across 68 genomes from vertebrate animals. They found that parts of the proteins, called the SCAN domain, have roots in an ancient retrovirus. The family later expanded independently in different animal groups, including mammals and lizards and snakes. Its DNA-recognizing regions also changed, allowing different proteins to recognize different transposable-element sequences.
To understand what these proteins do, the team then mapped where SZFPs attach to human DNA. They found that most of those targeted DNA sequences came from transposable elements. Meanwhile, they also found that different SZFPs evolved to recognize different pieces of transposable-element DNA, suggesting that the proteins and their genomic targets evolved alongside each other.
A different way to keep DNA closed
DNA is not loose inside a cell. It winds around proteins and folds into a material called chromatin. Some stretches are open enough for proteins to reach genes and help switch them on. Others stay tightly packed and less accessible. Scientists already knew that cells could keep DNA closed by adding chemical marks that help maintain a silenced state. The EPFL team found that SZFPs work without relying on these familiar marks.
To see how, the researchers temporarily removed one of these proteins from cells. Within hours, DNA around its usual binding sites became easier to access, and proteins involved in switching genes on began entering those regions. When the missing protein returned, the DNA closed again. Further experiments showed that opening required the cell's normal chromatin-rearranging machinery. A related protein showed a similar effect in mouse stem cells.
The findings suggest that SZFPs keep selected stretches of DNA inaccessible, rather than silencing them through the usual chemical tagging system.
Protecting genes while allowing change
The authors describe SZFPs as "guard" transcription factors. They appear to prevent proteins that open DNA from gaining access to particular regulatory regions. Unlike some conventional silencing mechanisms, their effects appear more local, potentially helping cells control one DNA switch without shutting down a larger surrounding region.
The findings also offer an evolutionary perspective. Transposable elements can introduce new genetic switches, some of which are later adopted by the host genome. By controlling when these switches become accessible, proteins such as SZFPs may help preserve established gene programs while contributing to differences in gene regulation between species.
"Repurposing the regulatory elements derived from transposable elements dramatically changes genetic programs and thus may contribute to the emergence of biological features specific to certain species," says Matsushima.
