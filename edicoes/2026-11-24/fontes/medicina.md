Can AI expedite medical imaging? Yes—if we can trust the picture
When it comes to medical imaging, everyone wants better technology faster—but no one wants to cut corners on health and safety. Enter a new statistical tool that determines whether AI-generated data could accurately stand in for patient data in clinical trials.
Jointly developed by researchers from Virginia Tech; the University of Maryland, Baltimore County; Texas Tech University; and Princeton University, the model was published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
Cutting trial timelines
Deploying a more powerful, precise MRI in a hospital, for instance, requires approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA). To prove these devices are safe and effective, manufacturers must run clinical trials—research studies using human subjects that can take years and cost millions of dollars.
To accelerate this process, the FDA is exploring AI-generated synthetic data alternatives that could compress trial timelines to a matter of months.
"The problem is, you have to verify that the synthetic data is true to life," said Yulia R. Gel, Virginia Tech professor of statistics. "We want clinical trials with synthetic data to reach the same conclusions we would get with real people."
The tool developed by Gel and her colleagues bridges two fields—computational topology and statistics—to measure and compare complex image data.
To test the method, the team compared synthetic mammograms against real patient data, tracking how breast cancer responds to different radiation doses. The proposed method enabled the researchers to assess the faithfulness of the synthetic images as a replacement for human data in trial evaluations.
But the research won't stop with publication. Gel and her team are already in conversation with the FDA to customize the model for streamlined premarket and postmarket evaluations.
Stress-testing AI across industries
The model's applications could reach far beyond medicine. The researchers have already applied it in materials science to verify synthetic representations of wood, fabric and metal.
"AI applications are vast and varied," said statistics graduate student Jaidev Goel, one of the leading authors on the paper. "It's being used to generate new protein structures and new compounds in drug discovery. A materials scientist may want to make something with the properties of cotton. We can use this tool to make sure it truly does have the properties of cotton—its density or texture."
Down the road, the model could verify synthetic data for power grids and 3D objects or help spot false assertions and restrict behavior in large language models.
"We shouldn't blindly buy into the hype of AI," Goel said. "We have always come up with ways to stress-test new tools. That's the crucial role statistics plays in human history."
