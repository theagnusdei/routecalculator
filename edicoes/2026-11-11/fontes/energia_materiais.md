## Texto extraído

New research explains why silicon outperforms thin film in the race for solar panel supremacy
Even the most promising technologies can fail to take off if governments, producers and suppliers, along with consumer and business demand, are not able to support their growth, according to new research by Bayes Business School.
The paper published in Organization Science, co-authored by Dr. Mara Guerra, senior lecturer in strategy at Bayes, and Professor Rajshree Agarwal of the University of Maryland, compared respective development pathways for silicon and thin-film technologies that grew the U.S. solar photovoltaics (PV) industry between the 1950s and the present day.
For many years, both technologies had their own strengths. Thin film had lower per-unit cost and versatility while silicon also benefited from falling costs and innovation, and there was no obvious front-runner. However, when demand finally took off in the late 2000s, silicon was in a better position to meet utilities' requirements at scale, reliably and cost-effectively.
The virtuous cycle of events that pressed home silicon's advantage is identified by the researchers as a "reinforcement loop." Established supply chains and manufacturing equipment helped silicon achieve scale, which then enhanced reliability, reputation and cost efficiency. This in turn attracted further interest, investment and demand.
By contrast, thin film had more customized supply chains and weaker economies of scale, making it less reliable and financially unsustainable and leading to a queue of market exits from investors and producers. Thin-film technology, according to the study, had been slowed down by "balancing loops."
At the end of the study period in 2020, silicon accounted for 91% of solar PV industry sales. While a similar number of solar cell producers entered into each technology during the period studied, three-quarters of thin-film companies exited—paving the way for silicon to dominate.
The research comprehensively explored a large range of industry news coverage, reports, market entrants' strategies, government interventions and investment history to uncover patterns and interdependencies with the fortunes of the two competing solar panel technologies.
Guerra said, "Our research strongly suggests that technologies do not just appear through innovation, but rely on interaction between a much wider group of actors, from manufacturers and equipment suppliers to governments, utilities and financiers.
"Silicon did not simply win the battle because it was the better technology. It won because it was better equipped to take advantage of an ecosystem that had developed around solar power as demand began to take off.
"Silicon solar PV's success has important wider implications for other emerging technologies. Developing a viable technology is only a small part of the challenge. Businesses and policymakers also need to understand whether the wider ecosystem—investors, activists, policymakers, consumers—is ready to support it at scale."
Publication details
Mara Guerra et al., Technology (Non)Emergence: How System Interdependencies Among Activities by Heterogeneous Actors Shaped Alternative Solar Technology Trajectories, Organization Science (2026). DOI: 10.1287/orsc.2022.17185
