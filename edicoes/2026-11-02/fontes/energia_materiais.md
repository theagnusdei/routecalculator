Scientists shrink Archimedesâ screw to the size of a single molecule
- Date:
- November 1, 2026
- Source:
- University of Augsburg
- Summary:
- Scientists have built a groundbreaking molecular motor that spins in either direction using electricity, inspired by a machine invented more than 2,000 years ago. Even more remarkably, its rotation can boost electrical conductivity by up to 100 times, potentially transforming the future of molecular electronics.
- Share:
Scientists have developed a remarkable new molecular motor that spins when electricity passes through it. Inspired by an ancient invention, the tiny machine can even reverse its direction when the electrical voltage is switched. The achievement marks an important step toward creating working machines at the smallest possible scale.
Molecular machines are widely seen as a promising next frontier in miniaturization. Nature already provides examples inside living cells, where molecular motors use energy to produce movement. Researchers have spent decades trying to recreate these remarkable biological systems artificially, and progress in this field was recognized with the 2016 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
An Ancient Invention Inspires a Molecular Motor
Scientists first developed molecular motors powered directly by electricity in 2011. However, those earlier designs operated on surfaces and relied on the tip of a scanning tunneling microscope to drive their movement.
The new motor takes a different approach, borrowing its basic design from the Archimedes screw, one of the oldest machines in recorded history. Traditionally used to move water uphill, this simple device relies on a rotating spiral to transport material.
Researchers adapted that principle to the molecular scale. Instead of moving water, their design allows electrons to travel through a spiral-shaped molecule, causing it to rotate as electrical current passes through.
A Tiny Molecular Screw Suspended Between Gold Tips
The motor's central component is a specially designed spiral molecule created by the research group of Prof. Michael Pittelkow at the University of Copenhagen. Measuring only a few millionths of a millimeter, the molecule can attach to gold surfaces through sulfur atoms.
At the Weizmann Institute of Science in Israel, researchers led by Prof. Oren Tal and Dr. Anil Kumar Singh positioned individual molecules between two gold tips sharpened to atomic dimensions. By carefully changing the separation between these electrodes, the team could stretch or compress the molecular screw.
When the scientists applied an electrical voltage, measurements revealed that the molecule began rotating. Even more importantly, they could reverse its direction simply by changing the voltage's polarity.
This represents the first demonstration of a molecular motor that is driven by electrical current, firmly connected to two metal electrodes, and capable of rotating in a controllable direction.
How Electricity Makes a Single Molecule Spin
The driving force comes directly from electrons traveling through the spiral molecule. As they move, they transfer angular momentum to the molecular structure, producing rotation in much the same way that moving air turns a wind turbine.
The spiral's shape determines how it responds to this flow, similar to the difference between screws with right- or left-handed threads. Once the applied voltage exceeds a certain threshold, the movement becomes continuous rotation.
But the experiments revealed another intriguing effect. The rotating molecule conducted electricity much more efficiently than it did when stationary, raising an important question about what was happening inside the tiny machine.
Scientists Discover a Surprising 100-Fold Electrical Effect
To investigate this unexpected behavior, Prof. Dr. Fabian Pauly and his PhD students Léon Martin and Yuta Ito, working at the Chair of Theoretical Physics I at the University of Augsburg, used quantum mechanical simulations on the LiCCA supercomputer.
Their calculations revealed a close relationship between the molecule's rotation and its ability to carry electrical current. Conductivity changes dramatically depending on the angle of the molecule, increasing by as much as two orders of magnitude, or 100 times, during rotation.
The reason turned out to be particularly surprising. Some of the molecular positions that allow electricity to pass most easily are also positions that the molecule naturally avoids when it is not moving. Rotation makes those highly conductive arrangements accessible.
"The surprising thing is that it is precisely the positions that the molecule avoids when at rest that conduct electricity best. It is only the rotation that makes these positions accessible," explains Léon Martin.
The finding demonstrates that molecular motion does more than simply consume electrical energy. The movement itself can strongly influence how electricity flows through the machine.
International Collaboration Solves the Mystery
The researchers then worked with theoretical physicists Per Hedegård of the Niels Bohr Institute in Copenhagen and Jing-Tao Lü of Huazhong University of Science and Technology in Wuhan.
Using the simulation results, the scientists developed a simplified theoretical model that successfully recreated both the rotation observed in the experiments and the accompanying increase in electrical current.
This combination of experimental measurements and theoretical calculations was essential to understanding the mechanism. Although researchers could measure the current passing through the molecule, they needed simulations to determine what was happening at such an extraordinarily small scale.
"In the experiment, you only see the current. Our calculations allow us to glimpse what is happening inside the nanocontact. It was only through close collaboration between experiment and theory across several countries that the mystery of the current increase could be solved," says Yuta Ito, a PhD student originally from Japan.
A New Frontier for Molecular Electronics
The achievement could eventually contribute to the development of molecular electronic components and technologies involving spintronics, which uses properties of electrons beyond their electrical charge. It may also offer possibilities for microfluidic systems, which manipulate extremely small quantities of fluids.
Beyond these potential applications, the motor provides scientists with a new way to investigate physical processes at the atomic scale. Its ability to convert electricity into controlled rotation, while allowing that rotation to influence electrical current, introduces a new principle for designing extraordinarily small machines.
"We have shown that an electric current can be used to generate a directed rotation and that this rotation, in turn, controls the current. This is a new operating principle for machines at the very limits of miniaturization," explains Pauly.
