In a first, ultrasound maps blood flow through an entire human kidney in 3D
For the first time, a research team at the Inserm Institute of Physics for Medicine (Inserm/ESPCI Paris-PSL/CNRS) in Paris has succeeded in visualizing the entire vascular network and blood flow in a human kidney. This breakthrough opens up promising clinical prospects: improving our understanding and diagnosis of kidney diseases linked to vascular defects. To achieve this, the researchers used a new ultrasound technique developed in their laboratory and previously tested in animals, which could eventually be extended to other organs. The results are published in The Lancet Medical Imaging & Theranostics.
The proper functioning of an organ depends closely on its vascular network. When arteries or veins are abnormal, the cells' needs for oxygen and nutrients are not met as effectively, and cellular waste is not eliminated as efficiently. The organs then suffer.
The kidney is particularly affected: changes in its microvasculature play a major role in many kidney diseases. This is particularly true of hypertensive nephrosclerosis and diabetic nephropathy, which are characterized by progressive damage to the small arteries and arterioles of the kidney, contributing to the loss of its filtration function.
"Currently, there is no specific biomarker that can directly detect these vascular abnormalities and monitor their progression. And the imaging techniques currently used cannot visualize the affected arterioles, whether it be conventional ultrasound—which lacks sufficient resolution—or MRI and CT scans," explains Clément Papadacci, an Inserm research fellow and team leader at the Institute of Physics for Medicine (Inserm/ESPCI Paris-PSL/CNRS).
Consequently, in the absence of early diagnosis and intervention, the disease is a major contributor to the development of kidney failure, requiring patients to undergo dialysis.
Directly observing the small blood vessels involved in kidney diseases is therefore a major challenge for clinicians. It is for this purpose, among others, that the team has developed a new ultrasound technique in recent years. By combining several innovations, they have developed an ultrasound probe that makes it possible to visualize the entire vascular system of an organ in three dimensions—down to the smallest capillaries—and to track blood flow in real time.
This is a remarkable achievement, given that some vessels are 50 micrometers in diameter—the size of a fine hair—and that conventional ultrasound can only visualize vessels 1 to 2 millimeters in diameter, or the size of the head of a pin.
The team has already demonstrated the effectiveness of this tool in animals for visualizing the vascular system of kidneys—and even entire hearts and livers. Now, they have demonstrated its feasibility in humans.
For the first time, the team is providing images of entire kidneys along with quantitative data on the organ's complete vascular architecture—from the largest arteries down to the microvessels—and the blood flow within each vessel. The system also allows the organ to be segmented to focus on areas of interest, or to separate venous and arterial blood flow for separate study.
"Beyond its technological capabilities, this new generation of ultrasound technology could lead to significant advances in research and medicine. In the case of hypertensive nephrosclerosis in particular, our device could help us not only better understand the condition but also, likely, identify early biomarkers to improve diagnosis," explains Hannah Plath, a doctoral student at the Institute of Physics for Medicine (Inserm/ESPCI Paris-PSL/CNRS) and the study's first author.
This new generation of ultrasound could well contribute to our understanding of other types of diseases, since, in principle, all organs can be observed in their entirety and with their full vascular complexity.
In a clinical trial, the team will monitor blood flow in the kidneys, as well as in the brain and heart, of patients with atherosclerosis who are candidates for complex surgery.
"This surgical procedure alters blood flow and can compromise these organs. The clinical study aims to predict potential risks for patients," explains Papadacci, who led the research.
How does ultra-high-definition ultrasound imaging of an entire organ work?
To visualize an entire organ with high precision, researchers have developed an ultrasound probe that is much larger than a conventional probe and incorporates numerous components. Acoustic lenses, in particular, are responsible for focusing sound waves with great precision. This probe makes it possible to explore a large volume in three dimensions with high resolution thanks to very high-speed image acquisition.
Finally, microbubbles injected into the bloodstream and carried along by blood flow reveal even the smallest vessels. The combination of these innovations thus makes it possible to achieve two capabilities that were previously difficult to combine: viewing an entire organ and distinguishing its microvasculature.
