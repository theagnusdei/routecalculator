Salty lake water helps a zinc battery keep working at −50°C
Extreme cold can affect a water-based battery's ability to deliver electricity. Its liquid electrolyte—the fluid that carries charge—can freeze, while the charged particles moving through it slow down. Researchers have now built a rechargeable zinc battery that works at −50°C (−58°F) using saline lake water as a starting ingredient.
The brine was modified with an antifreeze additive that kept it from freezing, and a protective metal coating helped the zinc electrode withstand its salty surroundings. Together, these changes produced a pouch battery that retained 80% of its capacity after 900 charging and discharging cycles at −50°C.
The team included Huajun Tian and colleagues from the North China Electric Power University, along with researchers from Qinghai Minzu University and Beijing University of Chemical Technology. Their findings, reported in the Journal of the American Chemical Society, outline an approach that could make use of an abundant, naturally salty water supply while reducing the need for purification.
The salts help—and hurt
Water-based zinc batteries commonly use deionized water, which has almost all of its dissolved mineral ions removed to give researchers greater control over the chemistry. Salty lake water arrives with those charged particles already present.
The mixture offers a head start against freezing. Dissolved salts make it harder for water molecules to organize into ice, and their ions help carry charge through the liquid. Instead of an involved purification process, the authors describe preparing the lake water as "requiring only simple collection and filtration procedures."
But some of the dissolved ingredients, particularly chloride, encourage corrosion and unwanted reactions at the zinc surface. As the battery recharges, zinc can also accumulate unevenly, growing dendrites that can eventually cause a short circuit. How could the researchers retain the salts' useful properties while limiting the damage?
To explore the possibilities, they mixed equal volumes of lake water and propylene glycol, an antifreeze ingredient, then added a zinc salt. Tests and simulations indicated that the mixture changed how water molecules interacted with one another and with zinc ions, helping prevent freezing and reduce unwanted reactions involving water.
The electrode needed protection too, so the researchers coated it with an alloy containing indium, tin and zinc. Its microscopic, coral-like structure encouraged zinc to deposit more evenly on the surface. Under a microscope, bare zinc developed branching deposits and released hydrogen gas, while the alloy-coated electrode maintained a smoother surface with less gas production.
Putting the protection to work
At −50°C, the coating and electrolyte lasted more than 3,200 hours in a test that repeatedly moved zinc between two electrodes at a low current. This tested the stability of the zinc surfaces but did not measure the lifespan of a complete battery. For the complete device, the researchers paired the protected zinc with an electrode made from polyaniline, a conducting polymer. The pouch version reached 900 cycles at −50°C while retaining four-fifths of its capacity.
There was a trade-off, however. Some ions naturally present in the lake water competed with zinc ions for places inside the polyaniline electrode, reducing the charge it could store.
Scaling up the new battery will also require a clearer accounting of manufacturing costs, including the antifreeze and alloy coating. For now, the laboratory cells show that keeping the water's natural salts can be useful, provided the rest of the battery is designed to tolerate them.
