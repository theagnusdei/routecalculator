## Texto extraído

A small galaxy's giant dark matter core challenges the standard cosmological model
New findings show that a nearby dwarf galaxy, UGCA 320, may challenge some predictions of the widely accepted cosmological model of the universe. By combining observations from the MUSE instrument with radio measurements from the MeerKAT telescope, astronomers have found that it hosts an unusually large dark matter core. The paper was posted to the arXiv preprint server on Nov. 11.
Cuspy vs. core
The standard cosmological model, called Lambda Cold Dark Matter, predicts that dark matter accumulates in a sharp "cusp" at a galaxy's center. Its density rises sharply toward the center. But observations of small galaxies have shown a flat, smooth "core" instead. This mismatch is called the cusp-core problem.
To address this problem, instead of "cold" dark matter, which moves slowly and interacts through gravity, various alternative dark matter candidates have been put forward. They are warm dark matter, self-interacting dark matter and fuzzy dark matter. There's also another explanation involving ordinary matter, which says that repeated bursts of star formation and supernovae can churn up the center and push dark matter outward, flattening the cusp.
In this new study, a team of researchers led by Qinwei Yuan of Nanjing University, China, studied gas-rich UGCA 320 to measure how mass is distributed in it. They wanted to check whether it is cuspy or has a core profile.
Researchers combined radio data from the MeerKAT telescope with optical light from the MUSE and WiFeS instruments and infrared images from Spitzer. Radio data trace the galaxy's neutral hydrogen distribution; optical data trace hydrogen-alpha glow emitted by gas near star-forming regions; and infrared data show the location of the stars.
What fits the best?
Using a three-dimensional "tilted-ring" model, where the galaxy is treated as a stack of concentric rings, each with its own tilt and speed, they measured how fast the galaxy is spinning at a given distance from the galactic center to get the "rotation curve." They estimated how much of that rotation is coming from the gas and stars. After subtracting the normal matter, what remains should come from the dark matter.
Then they fit the residual rotation curve to dark matter models: the LCDM-derived Navarro–Frenk–White dark matter halo (a cuspy shape), pseudo-isothermal and Burkert models (cored shapes), and a generalized Navarro–Frenk–White model—a flexible version that measures how cuspy or cored the center really is.
They found that the observations are poorly described by the cuspy profile. Instead, models with a flatter "core" distribution provide a better fit. UGCA 320's dark matter is spread out flat in the middle. The two best-fit models gave dark matter core radii of 7.62 kiloparsecs (24,850 light-years) and 11.97 kiloparsecs (39,000 light-years).
"Such a large dark matter core radius is uncommon among dwarf galaxies," the team writes in the paper. The region that stars occupy in UGCA 320 has an effective radius of about 2 kiloparsecs (6,600 light-years). That measures the size of the region containing half of the galaxy's starlight, so it gives an idea of how big the stellar body is. Clearly, according to the models, the dark matter core extends far beyond the galaxy's stellar body.
Warm, fuzzy or self-interacting?
The team tested several explanations for UGCA 320's overly extended dark matter core. Warm dark matter (WDM) is made of lighter and faster particles and can make cores. But here, it works only if the WDM particle mass is about 0.003 keV. Other observations, however, rule out WDM particles lighter than roughly 3 to 6.6 keV, so warm dark matter fits poorly.
Fuzzy dark matter (FDM) is made of ultralight particles with wavelike behavior. It also fits the shape of the core but, again, does not fall into the required particle mass range.
Next comes self-interacting dark matter (SIDM). In this case, particles bump into each other, transmit kinetic energy from outer to inner regions and spread the center out. This fits best. The interaction strength needed for this kind of dark matter falls in the range other studies find for galaxies of this size.
They found that the normal matter scenario involving star formation episodes and supernovae also falls short. Repeated star-formation explosions and infalling clumps can flatten a cusp, but simulations show that they can only make cores of about 0.1 to 2 kiloparsecs, not in the range of 7 to 12 kiloparsecs as seen here. Existing studies have not yet shown whether supernovae could produce cores this large in a small galaxy like UGCA 320.
Therefore, self-interacting dark matter is the most promising explanation, which may pose a challenge to the standard model based on cold dark matter. "Our results are broadly consistent with the SIDM scenario, while appearing to be in tension with WDM and FDM predictions," the team concludes.
