## Texto extraído

Why fertile male killifish outlive their peers
Why do some individuals of the same species live significantly longer than others? In evolutionary biology and aging research, this question is often linked to a trade-off between reproduction and survival. The underlying idea is that energy and resources invested in reproduction may lead to compromises in repair and maintenance, thereby hindering a long life. Thus, the assumption is that particularly successful reproduction at a young age comes at the cost of a shorter lifespan.
Researchers at the Leibniz Institute on Aging–Fritz Lipmann Institute (FLI) in Jena, Dr. Silvia Cattelan and professor Dario Riccardo Valenzano, have now investigated this hypothesis in more detail and examined the question using the turquoise killifish (Nothobranchius furzeri), a naturally short-lived fish. This fish is increasingly adopted as a model organism for aging research because it allows researchers to study the in vivo biology of aging within a period of just a few months.
The study results have now been published in Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London B: Biological Sciences, as a part of a special issue entitled "Developmental circuits in ageing."
In order to assess reproductive performance across different life stages, the researchers studied male turquoise killifish using a systematic approach. To this end, ejaculates were collected repeatedly throughout the animals' lifespans, and actual reproductive performance was determined after in vitro fertilization. In a subsequent step, the reproductive outcomes were correlated with individual lifespan. In addition, the researchers developed a comprehensive statistical model that recapitulates and helps interpret the relationship between reproduction and survival. The age-related decline in reproductive performance was also taken into account.
"It's been great fun to work on this project with Silvia—we had extensive discussions about the results that at first were puzzling and were challenging a long-standing dogma. The most important results could only be obtained through the combination of Silvia's expertise in male fish reproductive aging and my modeling approach using a Bayesian causal inference framework," explains Valenzano, group leader and scientific director at the FLI. The model was able to largely reproduce the observed results when it accounted for a common intrinsic quality as a factor influencing reproduction and lifespan.
Early reproductive success predicts a longer life
The study contradicts the assumption that reproductive success early in life predicts a short lifespan. In the male fish studied, higher reproductive success at an early age was an honest predictor of higher father quality. In fact, fathers with higher reproductive success in early life lived longer on average.
"What's striking about our finding is that since father reproductive fitness predicts lifespan, measuring reproductive success in young fathers could be used as a bona fide proxy for Darwinian fitness," explains Valenzano.
Individual biological quality as a key factor
The researchers therefore examined an alternative explanation: Individuals may differ in their intrinsic biological quality. If this quality is sufficiently high, they can reproduce more successfully. Conversely, these same individuals apparently also have better prospects for longer survival. Reproductive success and lifespan are thus two characteristics that are partly influenced by shared biological factors. In this context, reproductive performance could be understood as an indicator of general individual quality.
Whether the results obtained from male turquoise killifish can be directly extrapolated to females, other species or even humans must be clarified in further studies.
"What I found exciting is that just by using the ejaculate of these males, we can capture their overall quality as individuals. We still don't know what this quality is made of, and finding out, in males as well as in females, is one of the next questions we want to address," says Cattelan, first author of the study.
Search for common biological causes
The results suggest that differences in lifespan should not be understood exclusively as the result of a trade-off between reproduction and self-preservation. Rather, some of the differences could be due to the fact that certain individuals have more favorable biological conditions from the outset—or throughout their lives. Reproductive success is then not necessarily the cause of a longer lifespan, but rather a measurable indicator of an individual's differing initial biological quality.
This would also have broader implications for aging research: If we wish to understand why some individuals live longer and healthier lives than others, we may need to focus more on identifying common biological causes underlying health, reproductive capacity and survival.
