## Texto extraído

Insider Brief
- QuEra Computing and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory researchers have published a Nature study using the Aquila neutral-atom quantum computer to investigate how quantum systems evolve after sudden changes.
- Experiments involving arrays of up to 180 atoms revealed long-lived prethermal states, resonant melting and a first-order dynamical phase transition without a direct equilibrium counterpart.
- The team combined Aquila experiments with classical calculations on NERSC’s Perlmutter supercomputer to validate results and investigate quantum dynamics at larger scales.
PRESS RELEASE — The National Energy Research Scientific Computing Center (NERSC) at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and QuEra Computing, the leader in neutral-atom quantum computing, today announced the publication of a new study in Nature. Using Aquila, QuEra’s analog neutral-atom quantum computer, coupled with classical calculations on NERSC’s Perlmutter supercomputer, the team observed new physical phenomena, specifically how a quantum system settles after a sudden change.
The paper increases the scientific community’s understanding of processes that govern materials at the quantum scale. Classical simulation of these phenomena in large systems scales poorly, due to the fast growth of quantum entanglement with evolution time. A programmable quantum computer sidesteps this challenge by using the quantum states of the atomic qubits to model the target system and its evolution. A better understanding of how larger quantum systems evolve can inform materials science and more.
How QuEra and Berkeley Lab Worked Together
The study was co-designed by QuEra and NERSC scientists. NERSC ran exact classical calculations on Perlmutter, using QuEra’s open-source Bloqade software, for small probing systems, and those results were used to construct the quantum computer experiments. The NERSC team accessed Aquila on Amazon Braket, AWS’s quantum service.
“NERSC is focused on giving scientists access to useful computational tools. As quantum computing continues to advance, exact classical calculations are essential for validating what these machines tell us,” said Katie Klymko, NERSC Quantum Adviser. “In this work, calculations on Perlmutter gave us confidence in Aquila’s results at small scales, so we could trust the new physics it revealed at larger ones.”
The paper is the latest peer-reviewed result in a long and productive research collaboration between the Department of Energy’s National Labs and QuEra. A team from Los Alamos National Lab and QuEra published results on a new quantum error correction architecture, Transversal STAR, earlier this year.
What the Team Found
On Aquila, atomic qubits are initialized into a state in which every atom in the array starts in its lowest-energy state, a laser field switches on, the atoms evolve for a few millionths of a second, and every qubit is measured. Repeated across many settings and array shapes, from chains of up to 52 atoms to two-dimensional grids of up to 180, it produced three results.
- Long-lived intermediate states. Rather than settling smoothly, the system held distinct, stable patterns far longer than expected, a behavior known as prethermalization.
- Resonant melting. Those prethermalization patterns break down at sharp, predictable points, and the team explained quantitatively where and why.
- A two-dimensional transition with no equilibrium counterpart. In two dimensions, the system’s response changed sharply, and the change grew sharper as the array grew. The authors identify this as a first-order dynamical phase transition and find that it has no direct equilibrium correspondence.
“We usually think of phase transitions as changes in the state of matter, like water freezing into ice. But quantum matter can also undergo abrupt changes, including in how it evolves over time. Using Aquila, we discovered such a “dynamical” phase transition far from equilibrium, with no corresponding transition in the equilibrium phase diagram.” Said Milan Kornjača, Senior Quantum Scientist at QuEra and paper co-author. “This demonstrates how programmable quantum computers can reveal and explain fundamentally new physics. What excites me most is imagining what else we might discover as fault-tolerant quantum computers allow us to explore far beyond the constraints of analog computers.”
Science on Aquila
This is the latest in a substantial body of peer-reviewed research produced on Aquila, which has been available on Amazon Braket since 2022 as the first neutral-atom quantum computer on the service. Analog quantum simulation is producing published science today. The experience of building and operating a research-grade machine for the scientific community is what QuEra carries into Libra, its first fault-tolerant quantum computer, expected on Amazon Braket in 2028.
