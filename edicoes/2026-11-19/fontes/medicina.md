## Texto extraído

Study shows a small change in dialysis mortality calculations could have big impacts for patient care
A dialysis center that receives a poor mortality rating one year could receive a better rating the next, even if nothing about its patient care has changed. That is one potential consequence of a recent change in how Medicare collects patient information, according to a new analysis led by researchers at Brown University's School of Public Health and Warren Alpert Medical School and published in JAMA Internal Medicine.
The change happened in July 2025 when U.S. Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services officials stopped collecting race and ethnicity information across several federal forms, including one completed when patients begin treatment for kidney failure. The data on race and ethnicity are among numerous social and medical factors used in a formula that helps the Medicare system evaluate dialysis centers, where patients with kidney failure receive lifesaving treatment that filters their blood.
It is not yet clear how Medicare will calculate future mortality ratings without that information or when the removal of race and ethnicity data might affect the ratings patients see, but researchers used historical data to understand what those changes could mean.
The team simulated the removal of race and ethnicity from mortality calculations and determined that the shift could significantly alter how dialysis centers are rated on patient mortality by making many look better on paper—particularly those that serve large numbers of Black patients and/or socially disadvantaged patients.
For example, the simulation found that 27% of centers serving the highest proportions of Black patients moved into a better-performing category when race and ethnicity were removed. At the same time, nearly 22% of centers serving the most socially disadvantaged patients also improved their rankings, while about 15% of centers that serve fewer patients from populations facing social or economic disadvantages received worse rankings.
"Our study suggests that the removal of race and ethnicity data is going to have a significant impact on a number of dialysis facilities and how they're being scored in this model, so we wanted people to understand that going forward," said Kalli Koukounas Green, lead author of the new study and a research scientist at Brown's Department of Health Services, Policy and Practice. "A patient might see these new mortality ratings and make their referral decision based on that, not knowing that the more favorable rating is due to a modeling change."
The researchers believe the findings are particularly relevant for doctors who use these ratings to make referrals, as well as for the dialysis facilities themselves, which may use the ratings as a guide for investing in quality improvement efforts.
"These mortality measures and these kinds of relative performance rankings for dialysis facilities are important and have high-stakes consequences," said the study's senior author, Dr. Amal Trivedi, a professor of health services, policy and practice and of medicine.
How calculations can affect care
The research team examined data from 6,895 dialysis centers nationwide between 2017 and 2020. They calculated how each center would be rated based on patient death rates, first using race and ethnicity information and then leaving it out.
Centers serving more patients facing social or economic disadvantages generally received worse ratings when race and ethnicity were included in the calculations. When that information was removed, the gap in ratings between those centers and centers serving fewer disadvantaged patients nearly disappeared.
The study included some limitations, including that researchers used older data to estimate how mortality ratings might change because the effects of Medicare's new data collection practices are not yet known. Researchers said future research should examine how much dialysis center ratings actually change once the new practices are reflected in Medicare's published ratings.
The findings also point to a larger issue across health care of how changes in government data collection can affect important health care decisions. Hospitals, nursing homes and other medical providers, for example, are also evaluated using quality ratings that patients and families may rely on when making decisions. As in this study, changes to how those ratings are calculated could make providers appear better or worse without reflecting actual differences in care.
"It's important for federal policymakers to be aware that when you change health data collection, it affects our ability to understand public health trends and measure clinical performance," Trivedi said.
