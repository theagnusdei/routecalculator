DNA reveals the secret lives of Hawaiʻi's aeʻo (Hawaiian stilt)
Long-legged, tuxedo-clad and famously noisy, the aeʻo, or Hawaiian stilt (Himantopus mexicanus knudseni), has a family life as complicated as any reality TV show. A new study led by University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa researchers, published in Ecology and Evolution, paired a decade of birdwatching with DNA sequencing to uncover a world of egg-dumping, secret infidelities and surprisingly close family ties among the newly downlisted species.
Finding clues
Aeʻo usually nest in pairs, but researchers observed unusual family arrangements, including three adults sharing a nest and multiple generations defending territories together.
Co-author Arleone Dibben-Young color-banded more than 250 stilts on Molokaʻi and tracked them weekly for a decade. Researchers from the UH Mānoa Hawaiʻi Institute of Marine Biology and the College of Tropical Agriculture and Human Resilience's (CTAHR) Department of Natural Resources and Environmental Management (NREM) analyzed DNA from 91 birds to investigate their family relationships.
One incubating female was not the chick's genetic mother. This was the first genetic confirmation of "egg-dumping" in a stilt species, in which a female lays an egg in another bird's nest. Several nest "siblings" were found to be unrelated, and one mated pair may be siblings. Only about half the family ties seen in the field held up genetically.
The dangers of inbreeding
"In most bird studies, DNA sorts out parents, siblings and unrelated birds cleanly. Here, so many birds share so much ancestry that a parent and a sibling can look nearly identical," said Robert Toonen, a geneticist at HIMB. "That difficulty is itself a warning sign of how inbred this population has become."
The findings come months after the aeʻo was federally downlisted from endangered to threatened. It remains listed as endangered at the state level, with fewer than 1,800 stilts statewide.
"Downlisting is something to celebrate, but it's not the finish line," said lead author Melissa Price, a professor and wildlife ecologist at NREM. "We need to continue restoring habitat to support growing populations."
"The federal downlisting of the aeʻo reflects years of partnership among agencies, landowners and communities, and studies like this one help us see what comes next," said Afsheen Siddiqi, state waterbird biologist with the Hawaiʻi Department of Land and Natural Resources, Division of Forestry and Wildlife. "Knowing how closely related our stilts are will help us decide where to restore wetlands and how to keep these populations growing into the future."
Publication details
Melissa R. Price et al., Breeding Behaviors and Kinship in the Endangered Hawaiian Stilt (Ae'o; Himantopus mexicanus knudseni ), Ecology and Evolution (2026). DOI: 10.1002/ece3.74297
