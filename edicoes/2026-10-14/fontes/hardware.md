## Texto extraído

On 12 October Applied Materials unveiled a multi-billion dollar research facility in Sunnyvale, California, at the heart of Silicon Valley. The company first announced in 2023 plans to invest US $4 billion in its Equipment and Process Innovation and Commercialization (EPIC) Center, but now says it will invest even more: about $5 billion. The boost is part of a wider growth in private investment that is both enlarging the U.S. semiconductor R&D sector and making it more complex.
The EPIC announcement follows others, in August, by two semiconductor firms. Memory chip maker Micron disclosed that it would invest $10 billion over ten years at its Boise, ID, memory research lab, and SK hynix said it will spend $4 billion on an advanced packaging facility in West Lafayette, Indiana. (Neither of those companies responded to IEEE Spectrum’s questions about how much of those funds would be for R&D.)
The U.S. federal government has encouraged investment in U.S. semiconductor research by chip giants based in friendly nations, such as TSMC and Samsung. It has also enacted tariffs on foreign components and put some of its own money into faltering domestic chips companies including Intel. But the real motive behind today’s wave of private investment, according to some analysts and executives, is that companies anticipate enormous profits from feeding the current AI frenzy. “The best explanation is probably not about policy or politics and probably more about a big jump in revenue growth driven by AI demand,” says Chris Miller of Tufts University, the author of the book Chip War.
AI Money, Tools Accelerate Chip-Making
Applied Material’s chief strategy officer Tristan Holtam agrees, “it’s really all about AI.” The firm estimates that the top ten semiconductor companies’ revenue has grown about 150 percent since 2021 thanks to AI demand and they have in turn raised their investment in R&D by 67 percent, he says. Those top semiconductor companies are collectively investing $67 billion this year in R&D, Holtam adds, dwarfing federal funding. Most of that federal money comes from the 2022 CHIPS and Science Act, which allocated $11 billion for R&D over several years.
While demand for AI-ready chips provides funding and motivation for more chip R&D, AI in the form of research tools is helping the people actually doing the R&D. Entegris, a semiconductor materials science company headquartered in Billerica, Mass., is applying AI in its early-stage research and design, says Olivier Blachier, the company’s Chief Innovation Officer. At his company, workers are defining performance goals for next-generation products and asking AI tools to suggest promising new materials to match. “If you’re an AI engineer, a chemist, a materials scientist, you can combine scientific understanding with AI-driven modeling and simulation to work backward from a target performance requirement,” he says. The company is also piloting AI for preliminary screening of potential new materials, similar to what the pharmaceutical industry does with new molecules, before trying to synthesize and evaluate materials in the lab.
At Applied Materials, Holtam reports that the company is making use of AI-enabled acceleration in new material discovery, generative design of equipment, algorithm and software development. “The timing is perfect for EPIC,” Applied Materials’s new lab devoted to equipment and processes, he declares.

Reshaping Chip R&D, Not Just Resizing It
Physical proximity is emerging as a key advantage for chipmakers and that is reshaping the industry’s geographic and facilities investments. The Japanese semiconductor packaging company Resonac opened its first R&D facility in the U.S. in April, for example. “These companies have a very strong reason to want to be close to their customers and close to their customers’ customers so they can make the right investments for the next five to ten years,” according to Miller.
The knowledge of how prototype chip-making equipment works does not move up and down the supply chain as easily as software. “The traditional way of working to get brand new technology into the hands of our customers, the chip makers, was to ship a prototype piece of equipment from our lab into their pilot line. That’s a kind of heart-in-your-mouth type of situation,” Holtam says.
“That was always a huge point of contention,” he adds. ”At what level of maturity we’re willing to allow a prototype outside of our building when the chip company is pulling on that as hard as possible.” Applied Material analyzed 15 years of its own internal R&D to look for the places likely to benefit from fewer such heart-stopping shipments and from more shoulder-to-shoulder lab work. Then it conducted pilot programs of its customer-in-the-lab approach and got what Holtam deemed “very, very encouraging results.”
That helped prompt Applied Material to invite top customers to set up shop inside the new EPIC lab. So far, TSMC, Samsung, Micron, Broadcom, and others have said yes. Even competing customers will get their own facilities under EPIC’s roof, with digital firewalls, physical barriers, and other security features. “We’re eliminating handoffs,” Holtam says, and making it easier for their own research staff to give customers a preview of what’s coming.
Applied Materials’ stated goal is for its EPIC center to reduce, by several years, the time it takes to go from early-stage discoveries to chip commercialization. That’s typically a 10-to-15 year process.
The U.S. Department of Commerce announced similar goals for big-tent semiconductor R&D facilities under the CHIPS Act, but those are now behind schedule or indefinitely paused. They might have helped startups get a leg up on the chip giants in a notoriously concentrated industry. Indeed, EPIC’s partnership announcements so far have been with other industry leaders, so startups may be at the back of the queue. Right now, generally, Blachier says, “small and medium companies have to cooperate with bigger companies to feed into the giants.”
Another of the ways the industry is seeking acceleration is by closer ties with universities. Universities also serve as a bridge to the U.S. national semiconductor research strategy since universities have long-running research funding partnerships with the federal government.
“There is a benefit in having longer-term horizons and supported investments,” Blachier says, referring to EU and some Asian countries’ national chip strategies, which have created successful pre-competitive research facilities such as imec. More facilities like that in the U.S., he says, would be “definitely useful, and tend to benefit the ecosystem.”
