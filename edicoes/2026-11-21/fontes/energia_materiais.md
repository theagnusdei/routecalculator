Where do ions go? Hidden differences inside next-generation battery materials
When a rechargeable battery operates, ions move into and out of its electrode materials. But where exactly do these ions settle inside the crystal structure? And does their location affect how well a battery performs? These questions are particularly important for next-generation batteries that use abundant elements such as sodium, magnesium and calcium instead of lithium.
A research team at Tohoku University has now revealed that these ions behave very differently, even when inserted into the same electrode material. Their findings show how the locations of individual ions and the structural changes around them can influence battery performance and durability.
"Understanding where ions settle inside an electrode material is essential for designing batteries that can store more energy and last longer," says Hongyi Li, assistant professor at Tohoku University's Institute for Materials Research.
Details of the findings were published in the journal Chemistry of Materials.
Mapping ions inside manganese dioxide
The researchers focused on hollandite-type manganese dioxide (α-MnO₂), a material containing tiny, one-dimensional tunnels within its crystal structure. These tunnels provide pathways for ions to enter and move through the material. However, simply having enough space for ions does not guarantee that they can move easily or that the surrounding crystal structure will remain stable.
To investigate these challenges, the team combined advanced electron microscopy at the Tohoku Center of Excellence for Microscopy (TCEM) at Tohoku University with X-ray absorption spectroscopy at NanoTerasu, a next-generation synchrotron radiation facility. This approach allowed them to examine both the locations of inserted ions and changes in the surrounding atomic structure and electronic states.
Magnesium insertion brings distortion and damage
The differences were striking. Sodium ions spread relatively uniformly throughout the particles and occupied positions near the centers of the tunnels. Magnesium ions also entered the particles but tended to accumulate near their surfaces, occupying positions away from the tunnel centers. This was accompanied by significant distortion of the surrounding crystal structure. Calcium ions, meanwhile, remained mainly near the particle surfaces, with little evidence of insertion into the interior tunnels.
X-ray absorption measurements revealed another important difference: magnesium insertion caused more pronounced changes in the electronic states of manganese near the particle surfaces, whereas sodium produced relatively uniform changes and calcium produced only minor changes.
The researchers also found that repeated magnesium insertion and extraction damaged parts of the tunnel structure near the particle surfaces, even though the overall crystal structure remained largely intact. This local damage was associated with material loss and declining battery capacity.
The findings highlight that designing better battery electrodes requires more than providing pathways for ions. Controlling where ions settle and how they affect the surrounding atoms may be just as important. These insights could help researchers develop more durable, higher-capacity cathode materials for next-generation rechargeable batteries.
